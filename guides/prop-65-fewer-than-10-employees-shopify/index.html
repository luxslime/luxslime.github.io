Prop 65 small-business exemption

Prop 65 with fewer than 10 employees: does a small Shopify store still need a warning?

Direct answer. California’s OEHHA says a business with fewer than 10 employees is exempt from Proposition 65’s warning requirements, and the count includes full-time and part-time employees on the date the exposure occurs. The exemption does not remove contract duties such as a supplier agreement that tells you to show a warning or to indemnify, so check those before you take any warning off a product page.

What OEHHA says about the exemption

OEHHA, the California agency that administers Proposition 65, answers on its business FAQ that a company is exempt from the warning requirements if it has fewer than 10 employees. The same FAQ says Proposition 65 applies to all businesses with 10 or more employees that do business in California, and that out-of-state internet retailers are not exempt on that basis.

Sources: OEHHA business FAQ and Are any businesses exempt? on the Proposition 65 Warnings website.

How the employee count works

The FAQ quotes 27 CCR § 25102(h): in computing the number, all full-time and part-time employees on the date the exposure occurs must be counted. The regulation defines “employee” by reference to California’s Unemployment Insurance Code section 621 and Labor Code section 3351, so who counts is a legal question, not a payroll shortcut.

The regulation’s own wording around the threshold varies between “ten or fewer” and “ten or more”. If your headcount is at or near 10, get advice before you rely on the exemption.

What the exemption leaves open

  • Contracts. OEHHA notes that a business under the threshold may still have contractual obligations or indemnification agreements related to Proposition 65 warnings. A supplier, marketplace or wholesale agreement can ask for a warning you would not otherwise owe.
  • A 60-day notice. OEHHA says a retail seller that learns of an exposure only through a 60-day notice has five business days to correct the alleged violation, by adding a warning or by stopping sale of the product. Its small-business guidance says to check your contracts and your employee count, then respond to whoever sent the notice.

A checklist before you remove or skip a warning

  1. Count employees as the regulation does, for the date of the sale. Write the number and the date down.
  2. Read every supplier, marketplace and wholesale agreement for warning, compliance or indemnity clauses.
  3. Check whether a supplier has sent you warning text or a written notice for a product.
  4. Decide, product by product, whether to keep a warning anyway. Whether to show one anyway is a business decision to make with advice.
  5. If you keep or add one, approve the exact text and chemical name before it goes live, and keep the decision on file.
