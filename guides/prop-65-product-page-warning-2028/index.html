Prop 65 short-form warnings need a chemical name on 2028 product pages

Job: put a California Proposition 65 short-form warning on the product page that still qualifies as a safe-harbor warning after 1 January 2028. The old chemical-free short form (“Cancer and Reproductive Harm — www.P65Warnings.ca.gov”) is not that safe harbor for goods manufactured and labeled on or after that date. The new short form names at least one listed chemical.

This page is the product-page / short-form job, not a listing mirror and not legal advice. OEHHA’s amended Article 6 took effect 1 January 2025, with a three-year label transition through 1 January 2028. Confirm the live regulation before you ship copy.

Example new short-form sentences (safe-harbor options; insert the list name):

WARNING: Cancer risk from exposure to [name of chemical]. See www.P65Warnings.ca.gov.
WARNING: Can expose you to [name of chemical], a carcinogen. See www.P65Warnings.ca.gov.
WARNING: Risk of cancer and reproductive harm from exposure to [name of chemical]. See www.P65Warnings.ca.gov.

Signal-word alternatives include CA WARNING: and CALIFORNIA WARNING:. On-label short form also uses the yellow triangle symbol. This is a paraphrase of the approved text, not a substitute for 27 CCR §25603.

Do 2028 product-page short-form warnings need a chemical name?

For the current short-form safe harbor, yes when the product is manufactured and labeled on or after 1 January 2028. OEHHA’s amended Article 6 (effective 1 January 2025) requires the short-form text to name at least one listed chemical for each endpoint you are warning for (cancer and/or reproductive harm), unless one named chemical is listed for both. Use the chemical name as it appears on the Proposition 65 list. This page is not legal advice.

Can I keep the old chemical-free short form after 2028?

Only for products manufactured and labeled before 1 January 2028. Those goods may keep the prior short-form content (no chemical name) and still claim that older safe harbor, including unlimited sell-through of already-labeled stock. New manufacture and labeling on or after 1 January 2028 needs the chemical-identifying short form if you want that safe harbor. Confirm dates against OEHHA, not this page.

Does a product page warning have to match the on-product label?

Internet and catalog warnings are part of the same clear-and-reasonable warning rules. A product page that still shows the old chemical-free short form while the 2028-labeled unit names a chemical is the gap this page is about. During the three-year implementation period, retailers have a 60-day window to update online short-form warnings after notice from a manufacturer. Copy the warning content you actually use; do not invent a chemical name on the page that is not on the label.

Do I have to name every listed chemical in the product?

No. Safe-harbor short-form content requires the name of one or more listed chemicals for which the warning is being provided — at least one per endpoint if you warn for both cancer and reproductive harm, unless a single named chemical is listed for both. You choose which listed chemical(s) to name. The full list name is required; a nickname or CAS-only label is not the published safe-harbor form. This is not a substitute for counsel.

Is the old “Cancer and Reproductive Harm — www.P65Warnings.ca.gov” short form still a safe harbor?

That chemical-free short form remains available under the labeled-before-1-January-2028 transition (Section 25603(c) in the approved text). It is not the short-form safe harbor for products manufactured and labeled on or after that date. New short-form options name the chemical in the sentence, for example “Cancer risk from exposure to [name of chemical]. See www.P65Warnings.ca.gov.” Signal words may be WARNING, CA WARNING, or CALIFORNIA WARNING, plus the required yellow triangle symbol on the label path.

Does an app make the product page “Prop 65 compliant”?

No. Warning content is a legal choice. A listing can help you attach a cited template to a product, show it on the storefront, and keep an audit log of what you published. It does not decide which chemical to name, whether an exposure requires a warning, or whether your method is clear and reasonable. It is not legal advice and does not guarantee compliance.
