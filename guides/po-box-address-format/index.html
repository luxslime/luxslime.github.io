US PO Box address format

How a US PO Box address is written, and why checkout still sees other spellings

Direct answer. USPS Publication 28, section 281, says the Post Office Box address on a mailpiece must be standardized as PO BOX ##, where “##” indicates the actual number, numbers, or letter. That standardized line has no periods. Section 222 says that, with the exception of the hyphen in the ZIP+4 Code, punctuation may be omitted in the delivery address block, and that no punctuation is preferred. Section 284 says PO Box services in some locations allow an option to use the Post Office street address for the mailing address, along with the PO Box number preceded by a “#” sign or “UNIT” designation. usps.com calls that option Street Addressing, subject to availability and restrictions. Shoppers still type “P.O. Box”, “Post Office Box”, “POB”, “Box 12”, and locker lines. A checkout rule that matches only one of those strings will miss the others. Checklist before you write the line or the rule

  • Read Publication 28, section 281, for the standardized “PO BOX” plus the number.
  • Read section 283 for designations the Postal Service says to change to PO BOX on output.
  • Read section 222 before you treat punctuation as forbidden. The preferred form omits it, except the ZIP+4 hyphen.
  • Read section 284 and the usps.com PO Box page before you describe Street Addressing. It is an option in some locations, not a format every box has.
  • For the checkout spellings shoppers actually type, use the spelling-variants test list. This page does not repeat that list.

The standardized mailpiece line

Section 281 is titled Format, under “28 Post Office Box Addresses”. The sentence is: “The Post Office Box address on a mailpiece must be standardized as follows: PO BOX ## (in this example, ‘##’ indicates the actual number, numbers, or letter).” That is the delivery-address form Publication 28 standardizes. It is not “P.O. Box”, “Post Office Box”, or “POB”.

Section 282 says Post Office Box numbers that are preceded by significant leading zeroes are identified in the ZIP+4 file by a hyphen preceding the box number, and that the hyphen is converted into a zero on the output mailpiece. Section 283 says PO Box addresses often appear with the word CALLER, FIRM CALLER, BIN, LOCKBOX, or DRAWER, and that those designations are changed to PO BOX as output on a mailpiece.

Section 222, under the last line of the address, says punctuation may be omitted in the delivery address block except for the hyphen in the ZIP+4 Code, that an address with punctuation is acceptable, and that an address with no punctuation is preferred. The standardized “PO BOX” token in section 281 is already written without periods.

Street Addressing, where the Post Office offers it

Publication 28 section 284 is titled “PO Box Street Addressing”. It says PO Box services in some locations allow an option to use the Post Office street address for the mailing address, along with the PO Box number preceded by a “#” sign or “UNIT” designation. The usps.com PO Box page lists Street Addressing among PO Box features: a delivery address for packages from other carriers, including Amazon, DHL, FedEx, and UPS, subject to availability and restrictions. The same page’s question “What is Street Addressing and how can I get it for my PO Box?” says that, if available, you have the option of using the street address of your Post Office location, combined with your PO Box number, as your mailing address, so you can receive packages from private carriers such as UPS, FedEx, DHL, and Amazon, as long as they comply with USPS mailing standards. It points to the customer agreement for details. A street-style line can still be a PO Box. Whether a given box has the option is a location question on those USPS pages, not a guess from the words “PO BOX”.

Why the checkout line does not match section 281

People ordering online type the words they say: “P.O. Box”, “Post Office Box”, “POB”, a bare “Box 12”, or a locker line. Publication 28 tells mailers to standardize those kinds of designations to PO BOX on the mailpiece. A shopper at checkout has not done that. A rule that looks only for the letters P, O, space, B, O, X will miss “Post Office Box” and can also be confused by a real street.
